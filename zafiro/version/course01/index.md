## About this course

This course treats minerals and gems not as a catalogue of pretty stones, but as a **single discipline organized around eight domains** — mineralogy, crystallography, geochemistry, petrology, economic geology, gemology, materials science and environmental mineralogy. Each domain is climbed through the same six-layer stack:

```
         FEATURES         ← what the material + technique gives you
            ↑
         TECHNIQUE        ← how the material is found, identified, processed or treated
            ↑
         MATERIAL         ← what carries the property
            ↑
         DISCOVERY        ← the mineral, effect or law itself
            ↑
         MEASUREMENT      ← the instrument that first saw it
            ↑
         VOCABULARY       ← the terms that let you reason about all of the above
```

Mastering this stack across the eight domains gives you the ability to **identify a specimen**, **read its history**, **judge its value and use**, and **predict how it will behave** — in the ground, in a jeweller's tray, in a factory and in a river.

# Module 0 — Foundations

**Lesson:** [Lesson 9](Lesson9.md) · **Stack:** [course03 index](../course03/index.md) · [Pillar 1](../course03/p1-cycles-between-piles.md)

### Learning objectives
1. Define mineral, rock, gem, ore and material, and say how they overlap.
2. Describe the eight domains and the question each asks of a specimen.
3. Use the six-layer stack to organize what you learn in any domain.
4. Convert between wt%, ppm, g/t, carats, moles and volumes.
5. Set up a specimen log and a safe home kit.

### Key terms
Mineral · Rock · Gem · Ore · Material · Composition · Structure · Conditions (P–T–X) · Process · Rock cycle · Specimen · Provenance · Six-layer stack.

### Core content

**Five overlapping words**
- **Mineral** — a natural solid with defined composition and ordered structure (quartz).
- **Rock** — a natural aggregate of minerals (granite).
- **Gem** — a material cut or polished for beauty, rarity and durability (amethyst — quartz again).
- **Ore** — rock that pays to mine (a quartz vein with gold).
- **Material** — anything engineered for a use (silicon from quartz).

**The eight domains and their questions**

| # | Domain | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mineralogy | What is it? |
| 2 | Crystallography | How are its atoms arranged? |
| 3 | Geochemistry | Where did its elements come from, and when? |
| 4 | Petrology | What rock is it part of, and what made that rock? |
| 5 | Economic geology | Is it worth mining? |
| 6 | Gemology | Is it a gem — what, natural or not, treated or not, and how good? |
| 7 | Materials science | What can it be made into? |
| 8 | Environmental mineralogy | What happens when it meets air, water and life? |

**The six layers** — vocabulary, measurement, discovery, material, technique, features — are climbed in every domain (see [About this course](#about-this-course)).

**The three pillars** (course03): [cycles between piles](../course03/p1-cycles-between-piles.md); [composition + structure](../course03/p2-composition-and-structure.md); [conditions and scale](../course03/p3-conditions-and-scale.md).

### Try it (Module 0 exercises)
1. Is ice a mineral? Is a pearl? Is window glass? Say why for each.
2. Convert 1.2 wt% Zn into ppm and g/t.
3. A 2.5 ct emerald (SG 2.72): what is its mass and volume?
4. Pick any object in your kitchen and name the mineral it started as.
5. Write the eight questions for a piece of slate.

### Checkpoint
- [ ] You can explain the difference between a mineral, a rock and a gem with one example of each.
- [ ] You can name the eight domains and their questions from memory.
- [ ] You have a specimen log with at least five entries.

# Module 1 — Mineralogy Domain

**Lesson:** [Lesson 1](Lesson1.md) · **Stack:** [course03/01-mineralogy](../course03/01-mineralogy/README.md)

### Learning objectives
1. Define a mineral, a mineral species, a polymorph and a solid solution.
2. Identify common minerals by hardness, streak, cleavage, luster and SG.
3. Explain why color is unreliable and streak is not.
4. Name the mineral classes and the silicate subclasses by tetrahedron linkage.
5. Choose between hand tests, XRD, microprobe and Raman for a given question.

### Key terms
Mineral · Mineral species · Polymorph · Solid solution · Habit · Cleavage · Fracture · Luster · Streak · Hardness · Specific gravity · Silicate · Mineral class · IMA.

### Core content

A mineral is a naturally occurring solid with a definite chemical composition (or a defined range of compositions) and an ordered arrangement of atoms. Composition and structure fix its properties, so you identify a mineral by testing properties that cannot lie: hardness, streak, cleavage, luster and specific gravity. Color is the property beginners trust most and the one that lies most often.

- SG = W_air / (W_air − W_water) [dimensionless]
- Mohs hardness: an ordinal scratch scale, 1 talc … 10 diamond — not proportional

Mineral classes: native elements · sulfides · halides · oxides · carbonates · sulfates · phosphates · silicates (by tetrahedron linkage: isolated · rings · chains · sheets · frameworks).

### Discoverers & measurement

| Year | Person | What they measured | Instrument |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1546 | Agricola | Minerals described by external characters | Eye, hand, fire, simple assays (*De Natura Fossilium*) |
| 1774 | Werner | Systematic external characters — color, luster, hardness | Hand lens, steel, streak (*Von den äusserlichen Kennzeichen der Fossilien*) |
| 1812 | Mohs | Relative hardness | Scratch tests against ten reference minerals |
| 1810s | Berzelius | Chemical composition of minerals | Blowpipe analysis, wet chemistry |
| 1913 | W. H. & W. L. Bragg | Atomic arrangement of a mineral (halite) | X-ray spectrometer |
| 1951 | Castaing | Composition of micrometre-sized spots | Electron microprobe (EPMA) |

### Materials, techniques, features

| Function | Material | Technique | Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identify a species | Any mineral | Hardness + streak + cleavage + SG | Fast, free, non-destructive (mostly) |
| Confirm a species | Powder or crystal | XRD | Unique structural fingerprint |
| Map composition | Polished section | Electron microprobe | Micrometre-scale chemistry |
| Identify in place | Crystal, inclusion | Raman spectroscopy | Non-destructive; through glass |
| Separate by density | Mixed grains | Panning, heavy liquids | Concentrates dense minerals |

### Try it (Module 1 exercises)
1. Why is diamond hard but graphite soft, when both are pure carbon?
2. A mineral has SG 4.0, scratches quartz and does not cleave. Suggest a candidate.
3. Order olivine, pyroxene, mica and quartz by how their SiO₄ tetrahedra link.
4. Why does a red ruby and a blue sapphire count as the same mineral species?
5. Design a three-test key that separates calcite, quartz and fluorite.

### Checkpoint
- [ ] You can identify five common minerals from hand tests alone.
- [ ] You can measure SG to two significant figures with a kitchen scale.
- [ ] You can explain one polymorph pair and one solid-solution series.

# Module 2 — Crystallography Domain

**Lesson:** [Lesson 2](Lesson2.md) · **Stack:** [course03/02-crystallography](../course03/02-crystallography/README.md)

### Learning objectives
1. Define lattice, unit cell, motif, Miller indices and d-spacing.
2. Explain why only 1-, 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold rotations occur in crystals.
3. Name the 7 crystal systems and relate them to habit.
4. Apply Bragg's law to compute a peak position or a d-spacing.
5. Choose between powder XRD, single-crystal XRD and electron diffraction.

### Key terms
Lattice · Unit cell · Motif · Crystal system · Bravais lattice · Point group · Space group · Miller indices · d-spacing · Form and habit · Twinning · Defect · Coordination number · Anisotropy.

### Core content

A crystal is one small motif of atoms repeated on a lattice in three dimensions. The symmetry of that repeat decides the outer shape, which directions are equal, and how the crystal handles light, heat, stress and X-rays. Only 1-, 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold rotations fit a repeating lattice — that single constraint is why there are exactly 7 crystal systems, 14 Bravais lattices, 32 point groups and 230 space groups.

- Bragg's law: nλ = 2d sin θ
- Cubic d-spacing: d_hkl = a / √(h² + k² + l²) [Å]

Crystal systems: cubic · tetragonal · orthorhombic · hexagonal · trigonal · monoclinic · triclinic.

### Discoverers & measurement

| Year | Person | What they measured | Instrument |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1669 | Steno | Angles between faces of quartz crystals | Cross-sections and drawings of many crystals |
| 1780s | Carangeot; Romé de l'Isle | Interfacial angles of hundreds of crystal forms | Contact goniometer |
| 1809 | Wollaston | Interfacial angles to minutes of arc | Reflecting goniometer |
| 1912 | Friedrich, Knipping & von Laue | X-ray diffraction by a crystal (copper sulfate) | X-ray tube, photographic plate |
| 1913 | W. H. & W. L. Bragg | Structures of halite and diamond | X-ray spectrometer with ionization chamber |
| 1916 | Debye & Scherrer | Diffraction from powders | Powder camera (film) |
| 1982 | Shechtman | Ten-fold diffraction symmetry in an Al–Mn alloy | Electron diffraction in a TEM |

### Materials, techniques, features

| Function | Material | Technique | Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identify a phase | Powder of any crystalline mineral | Powder XRD | d-spacing fingerprint; works on mixtures |
| Solve a structure | Single crystal ~0.1 mm | Single-crystal XRD | Atom positions to ~0.01 Å |
| Read symmetry from shape | Well-formed crystal | Goniometry | No equipment beyond a protractor |
| Nano-structure | Thin foil | TEM electron diffraction | Defects, twins, nanophases |
| Orient a crystal | Single crystal | Laue back-reflection | Cut along a chosen plane |

### Try it (Module 2 exercises)
1. Why can no crystal have five-fold rotational symmetry — and why do quasicrystals not break the rule?
2. Calculate d₁₁₁ for halite (a = 5.64 Å) and its 2θ for Cu Kα.
3. Sketch the (100), (110) and (111) planes of a cube.
4. Explain why calcite shows double refraction and halite does not.
5. How many formula units are in the unit cell of diamond?

### Checkpoint
- [ ] You can index a simple cubic plane and compute its d-spacing.
- [ ] You can predict a powder XRD peak position from Bragg's law.
- [ ] You can explain how symmetry links atomic structure to external shape.

# Module 3 — Geochemistry Domain

**Lesson:** [Lesson 3](Lesson3.md) · **Stack:** [course03/03-geochemistry](../course03/03-geochemistry/README.md)

### Learning objectives
1. Use Goldschmidt's classes to predict where an element resides.
2. Explain substitution by ionic radius and charge.
3. Distinguish compatible from incompatible elements.
4. Calculate a radiometric age from a parent–daughter ratio.
5. Choose between XRF, ICP-MS and TIMS for a given question.

### Key terms
Major / minor / trace element · ppm, ppb · Lithophile / siderophile / chalcophile / atmophile · Compatible / incompatible · Partition coefficient · Ionic radius · Isotope · Radiogenic isotope · δ notation · Half-life · Closure temperature · Fractionation · Eh–pH.

### Core content

Elements go where their charge and size fit. Each element has an affinity — for silicate rock, metal, sulfide or gas (Goldschmidt's classes) — and it substitutes for ions of similar radius and charge in crystal structures. Follow those rules and you can predict where an element ends up. Radioactive isotopes add a clock: they decay at a fixed rate, so the ratio of daughter to parent tells you when a mineral closed.

- Radioactive decay: N = N₀ e^(−λt); half-life t½ = ln 2 / λ
- Age: t = (1/λ) · ln(1 + D*/P)
- Partition coefficient: D = C_mineral / C_melt

Goldschmidt classes: lithophile · siderophile · chalcophile · atmophile.

### Discoverers & measurement

| Year | Person | What they measured | Instrument |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1859–1861 | Bunsen & Kirchhoff | Element emission lines; discovered caesium and rubidium | Flame spectroscope |
| 1907 | Boltwood | Lead-to-uranium ratios as ages of minerals | Chemical analysis of uranium minerals |
| 1913 | J. J. Thomson | Two isotopes of neon | Positive-ray apparatus |
| 1924 | Clarke & Washington | Average composition of the Earth's crust | Thousands of wet-chemical rock analyses |
| 1920s–1930s | Goldschmidt | Distribution of elements among minerals and meteorite phases | X-ray and optical spectrography |
| 1956 | Patterson | Age of the Earth, ~4.55 billion years | Lead isotopes in meteorites; mass spectrometer |
| 1980 | Houk et al. | Trace elements at ppb levels | Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS) |

### Materials, techniques, features

| Function | Material | Technique | Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole-rock chemistry | Rock powder | XRF on fused bead | Major elements to ~0.1 wt% relative |
| Trace elements | Rock or mineral | Solution or laser-ablation ICP-MS | ppb detection; spot analyses |
| Dating | Zircon, monazite | U–Pb by TIMS or LA-ICP-MS | Ages from millions to billions of years |
| Palaeotemperature | Carbonate shells | δ¹⁸O by gas mass spectrometry | Past ocean temperatures |
| Water chemistry | Natural waters | Ion chromatography, ICP | Dissolved load, saturation state |

### Try it (Module 3 exercises)
1. Why does Rb substitute for K in feldspar but not for Si?
2. Convert 0.5 wt% Cu into ppm and g/t.
3. ⁸⁷Rb has a half-life of about 49 billion years. Why is Rb–Sr useful for billion-year-old rocks but useless for a 10 000-year-old lava?
4. Why is zircon better than feldspar for U–Pb dating?
5. Predict whether Ni goes into early olivine or stays in the melt.

### Checkpoint
- [ ] You can place ten common elements into Goldschmidt's classes.
- [ ] You can compute an age from a decay ratio.
- [ ] You can explain what a closure temperature means for a mineral clock.

# Module 4 — Petrology Domain

**Lesson:** [Lesson 4](Lesson4.md) · **Stack:** [course03/04-petrology](../course03/04-petrology/README.md)

### Learning objectives
1. Classify a rock as igneous, sedimentary or metamorphic from texture and minerals.
2. Explain Bowen's reaction series and what it predicts.
3. Convert depth to pressure and estimate temperature from a geothermal gradient.
4. Read metamorphic grade from index minerals.
5. Describe how a thin section is made and what it reveals.

### Key terms
Igneous: intrusive / extrusive · Felsic / mafic / ultramafic · Texture · Magma differentiation · Clastic / chemical / biogenic sediment · Diagenesis, lithification · Metamorphism: regional / contact · Foliation · Index mineral · Metamorphic facies · Thin section · Modal analysis · QAPF diagram.

### Core content

A rock is a record of where and how it formed. Igneous rocks cooled from melt, and their grain size records how fast; sedimentary rocks were deposited and cemented, and their grains record transport; metamorphic rocks recrystallized in the solid state under new pressure and temperature, and their mineral assemblage records how deep and how hot. Learn to read the minerals and the texture and the rock tells you its history.

- Lithostatic pressure: P = ρ g h — about 26.5 MPa per km for ρ = 2700 kg/m³
- Geothermal gradient: roughly 25–30 °C per km in continental crust
- Bowen's reaction series: olivine → pyroxene → amphibole → biotite; Ca-plagioclase → Na-plagioclase; then K-feldspar, muscovite, quartz

Rock families: igneous (intrusive · extrusive) · sedimentary (clastic · chemical · biogenic) · metamorphic (foliated · non-foliated).

### Discoverers & measurement

| Year | Person | What they measured | Instrument |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1788 | Hutton | Unconformity at Siccar Point; granite veins cutting older rock | Field observation |
| 1828 | Nicol | Polarized light from calcite | Nicol prism |
| 1858 | Sorby | Microscopic structure of rocks | Thin sections under the microscope |
| 1870s | Zirkel; Rosenbusch | Rock types by their minerals in thin section | Polarizing (petrographic) microscope |
| 1893 | Barrow | Metamorphic zones in the Scottish Highlands | Field mapping of index minerals |
| 1915 | Eskola | Mineral assemblages in metamorphic rocks of Orijärvi | Field mapping and microscopy |
| 1910s–1920s | Bowen | Order of crystallization in silicate melts | Quench-furnace experiments |

### Materials, techniques, features

| Function | Material | Technique | Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Name a plutonic rock | Granite, diorite, gabbro | Modal analysis + QAPF | Standard IUGS name |
| Read cooling history | Igneous texture | Grain-size observation | Fast vs slow cooling; two-stage (porphyritic) |
| Read P–T of formation | Metamorphic assemblage | Index minerals, facies, thermobarometry | Depth and temperature of burial |
| Read transport | Sandstone | Grain shape, sorting, composition | Distance and energy of transport |
| Map stability fields | Synthetic and natural samples | Furnace and piston-cylinder experiments | Phase diagrams |

### Try it (Module 4 exercises)
1. Why is basalt fine-grained and gabbro coarse-grained when they have the same composition?
2. Compute the pressure at 35 km (ρ = 2800 kg/m³).
3. Name the rock: coarse, 30% quartz, 40% K-feldspar, 20% plagioclase, 10% biotite.
4. Why does slate split into flat sheets?
5. Which weathers faster, olivine or quartz — and how does that connect to Bowen's series?

### Checkpoint
- [ ] You can name ten common rocks by sight with a hand lens.
- [ ] You can trace a rock through two steps of the rock cycle.
- [ ] You can estimate P and T of metamorphism from depth and gradient.

# Module 5 — Economic Geology Domain

**Lesson:** [Lesson 5](Lesson5.md) · **Stack:** [course03/05-economic-geology](../course03/05-economic-geology/README.md)

### Learning objectives
1. Define ore, grade, cut-off, tonnage and concentration factor.
2. Explain the difference between a resource and a reserve.
3. Describe the main deposit types and the processes that form them.
4. Calculate contained and recovered metal from tonnage, grade and recovery.
5. Describe how froth flotation separates minerals.

### Key terms
Ore · Gangue · Grade · Cut-off grade · Tonnage · Concentration factor · Resource / reserve · Deposit type · Hydrothermal fluid · Alteration halo · Beneficiation · Froth flotation · Tailings.

### Core content

An ore deposit is an ordinary element concentrated far above its crustal average by a geological process, in a place and at a grade where it pays to dig it out. Geology makes the concentration; economics decides whether it is ore. The same rock can be ore at one metal price and waste at another.

- Concentration factor = ore grade / average crustal abundance
- Contained metal = tonnage × grade; recovered metal = contained × recovery
- 1 g/t = 1 ppm

Deposit families: magmatic (chromite, PGE, Ni sulfide) · porphyry · VMS · sediment-hosted · orogenic gold · epithermal · placer · laterite · evaporite · kimberlite · pegmatite.

### Discoverers & measurement

| Year | Person | What they measured | Instrument |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1556 | Agricola | Mining, ore dressing and assaying practice | Fire assay, sluices, stamps (*De Re Metallica*) |
| 1858 | Sorby | Fluid trapped in minerals as a record of formation | Microscope on fluid inclusions |
| 1913 | Lindgren | Deposits classed by depth and temperature of formation | Field observation, ore microscopy (*Mineral Deposits*) |
| 1960s | Roedder | Temperatures and salinities of ore fluids | Heating–freezing microscope stage |
| 1979 | Alvin dive team, East Pacific Rise | ~350 °C metal-rich vents — black smokers | Submersible with temperature probe |

### Materials, techniques, features

| Function | Material | Technique | Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Find a deposit | Alteration halo, geochemical anomaly | Mapping, sampling, geophysics | Targets for drilling |
| Measure a deposit | Drill core | Assays, block model | Tonnage and grade with confidence levels |
| Concentrate sulfides | Chalcopyrite, galena, sphalerite | Froth flotation | Concentrate of ~25–30% Cu from <1% ore |
| Recover gold | Low-grade oxidized ore | Heap leach, cyanidation | Economic at ~1 g/t |
| Separate heavy minerals | Placer sands | Gravity (jigs, spirals), magnets | Cheap, no chemicals |

### Try it (Module 5 exercises)
1. Why can a rock be ore one year and waste the next?
2. Convert 3.2 g/t gold into ppm and into troy ounces per tonne (1 oz t = 31.1 g).
3. Which deposit type supplies most of the world's copper?
4. Why are placers rich in gold, cassiterite and diamonds but not in feldspar?
5. Estimate the tailings produced by a mine recovering 100 000 t Cu per year from 0.6% ore at 88% recovery.

### Checkpoint
- [ ] You can compute a concentration factor and contained metal.
- [ ] You can name the ore mineral for five common metals.
- [ ] You can explain one beneficiation method from crushing to concentrate.

- Snell's law: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂
- Critical angle: sin θc = 1 / n (gem in air)
- Mass: 1 carat = 0.200 g

Gem families by species: diamond · corundum · beryl · chrysoberyl · spinel · garnet · tourmaline · topaz · quartz · feldspar · jade · opal · organics (pearl, amber, coral).

### Key terms

### Core content

Minerals that formed hot and deep are out of equilibrium at the Earth's surface. Water, oxygen, carbon dioxide and microbes break them down to more stable phases — clays, iron oxides, carbonates — and along the way elements are released or trapped. How fast depends on surface area and solubility; where contaminants end up depends on sorption onto clays and iron oxides.

- Pyrite oxidation: FeS₂ + 7/2 O₂ + H₂O → Fe²⁺ + 2 SO₄²⁻ + 2 H⁺
- Saturation index: SI = log(IAP / K_sp) — dissolves if SI < 0, precipitates if SI > 0
- Goldich stability series: olivine weathers fastest, quartz slowest — the reverse of Bowen's series

Processes: dissolution · oxidation · hydrolysis · precipitation · sorption · biomineralization.

# Appendix E — Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 4Cs | Colour, clarity, cut, carat — the diamond grading system. |
| Acid mine (rock) drainage | Acidic, metal-rich water from oxidizing sulfides. |
| Acid potential / neutralization potential | The acid a rock can make (from its sulfide) vs the acid it can consume (from its carbonate). |
| Alteration halo | The zone of chemically changed rock around a deposit — often bigger than the ore and easier to find. |
| Anisotropy | Properties that depend on direction — cleavage, thermal expansion, refractive index. |
| Band gap | Energy between filled and empty electron states; sets semiconductor behaviour and colour. |
| Beneficiation | Upgrading ore before smelting — crushing, grinding, flotation, gravity and magnetic separation. |
| Bioavailability | The fraction of a contaminant that organisms can actually take up. |
| Biomineralization | Minerals made by organisms — shells, bones, microbial iron oxides. |
| Birefringence (Δn) | Difference between the highest and lowest RI. |
| Bonding | Ionic, covalent, metallic, van der Waals — sets hardness, melting point and conductivity. |
| Bravais lattice | One of the 14 distinct lattices in three dimensions (Bravais, 1848). |
| Carat | Unit of mass, 0.2 g (not to be confused with karat, the purity of gold). |
| Carbon mineralization | Locking CO₂ into carbonate minerals by reaction with Ca-, Mg- and Fe-rich rock. |
| Cation-exchange capacity (CEC) | How many cations a clay or soil can hold and swap; high for smectite, low for kaolinite. |
| Clastic / chemical / biogenic sediment | Made of fragments / precipitated from water / built by organisms. |
| Clay minerals | Sheet silicates such as kaolinite, illite and smectite; tiny, with huge surface area. |
| Cleavage | Breaking along flat planes of weak bonding; described by number of directions and angles. |
| Closure temperature | Temperature below which a mineral stops exchanging parent and daughter — when its clock starts. |
| Compatible / incompatible | Elements that enter early crystals (Ni, Cr) versus stay in the melt (K, U, Th, rare earths). |
| Concentration factor | How many times above crustal average the deposit grade is. |
| Coordination number | Number of nearest neighbours around an atom — 6 for Na in halite, 4 for Si in quartz. |
| Critical angle | Angle beyond which light is totally reflected inside the gem. |
| Crystal system | One of 7: cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, hexagonal, trigonal, monoclinic, triclinic. |
| Cut-off grade | The lowest grade that is mined as ore rather than sent to waste. |
| d-spacing | Distance between parallel lattice planes (hkl); what diffraction measures. |
| Defect | Departure from perfect order — point (vacancy, substitution), line (dislocation), planar (twin, grain boundary). |
| Deposit type | Porphyry, volcanogenic massive sulfide (VMS), sediment-hosted, orogenic gold, placer, laterite, evaporite, kimberlite, pegmatite. |
| Diagenesis, lithification | Compaction and cementation that turn sediment into rock. |
| Dislocation | A line defect whose motion makes metals ductile. |
| Dispersion ("fire") | Variation of RI with wavelength; splits white light into colours. |
| Dopant | A deliberate trace impurity that changes properties — Cr in ruby, B or P in silicon. |
| Eh–pH | Redox potential and acidity; together they decide which species of an element is stable in water. |
| Felsic / mafic / ultramafic | Rich in feldspar and silica / magnesium and iron / almost all olivine and pyroxene. |
| Foliation | Planar fabric from aligned minerals — slate, schist, gneiss. |
| Form and habit | A set of symmetry-equivalent faces; the overall shape that results. |
| Fractionation | Separation of elements or isotopes by a process (crystallization, evaporation). |
| Fracture | Breaking without planes — conchoidal (shell-like) in quartz and glass. |
| Fracture toughness (K_IC) | Resistance to crack growth; low for ceramics and glass. |
| Froth flotation | Separating minerals by making the valuable ones attach to air bubbles. |
| Gangue | The worthless minerals in an ore. |
| Glass transition | Temperature where a supercooled liquid becomes a rigid glass. |
| Grade | Concentration of the valuable component — % for base metals, g/t for precious metals. |
| Grain boundary | Interface between crystals of different orientation. |
| Habit | The usual shape a mineral grows in — cubic, prismatic, platy, fibrous. |
| Half-life (t½) | Time for half the parent atoms to decay; t½ = ln 2 / λ. |
| Hardness (Mohs) | Resistance to scratching, 1–10, by comparison with ten reference minerals. |
| Hydrolysis | Reaction of a silicate with water that releases cations and leaves clay. |
| Hydrothermal fluid | Hot water that dissolves, carries and deposits metals. |
| Igneous: intrusive / extrusive | Cooled from melt underground (coarse, e.g. granite) or at the surface (fine or glassy, e.g. basalt, obsidian). |
| IMA | International Mineralogical Association (founded 1958); approves new mineral species and names. |
| Inclusion | Anything trapped inside a gem — crystals, fluids, fractures, growth features. |
| Index mineral | A mineral whose first appearance marks a grade — chlorite, biotite, garnet, staurolite, kyanite, sillimanite. |
| Ionic radius | Effective size of an ion in a structure; substitution needs similar radius and charge. |
| Iron oxyhydroxides | Goethite, ferrihydrite, schwertmannite — the rust-coloured sponges of the environment. |
| Isotope | Atoms of one element with different numbers of neutrons. |
| Lattice | An infinite array of identical points; the repeat pattern without the atoms. |
| Lithophile / siderophile / chalcophile / atmophile | Goldschmidt's classes: elements that prefer silicates, metal, sulfides, gas. |
| Luster | How a surface reflects light — metallic, vitreous, pearly, resinous, earthy. |
| Magma differentiation | Changing melt composition as crystals form and separate. |
| Major / minor / trace element | >1 wt% / 0.1–1 wt% / <0.1 wt% (<1000 ppm). |
| Metamorphic facies | A set of assemblages that form under a given range of P and T (Eskola). |
| Metamorphism: regional / contact | Recrystallization over large areas during mountain building / around an intrusion. |
| Microstructure | Grains, phases and pores seen under the microscope. |
| Miller indices (hkl) | Integers naming a plane by the reciprocals of its axis intercepts. |
| Mineral | A naturally occurring, usually inorganic solid with a definite composition (or range) and an ordered atomic structure. |
| Mineral class | Grouping by the dominant anion: native elements, sulfides, halides, oxides, carbonates, sulfates, phosphates, silicates. |
| Mineral fibres and dusts | Asbestos, crystalline silica — hazardous when inhaled. |
| Mineral species | A mineral with a unique combination of composition and structure, approved by the IMA. |
| Modal analysis | Volume percentages of minerals, by point counting. |
| Motif (basis) | The group of atoms placed at each lattice point. |
| Ore | Rock from which a mineral or metal can be extracted at a profit. |
| Partition coefficient (D) | Concentration in a mineral divided by that in the melt or fluid. |
| Phase diagram | Map of stable phases versus temperature and composition. |
| Phenomena | Optical effects — asterism (stars), chatoyancy (cat's eye), play-of-colour (opal), adularescence. |
| Piezoelectricity | Charge from stress (and strain from voltage) in non-centrosymmetric crystals. |
| Pleochroism | Different colours seen along different directions in an anisotropic gem. |
| Point group (crystal class) | One of 32 combinations of symmetry operations that leave a point fixed. |
| Polymorph | Same composition, different structure — diamond and graphite (C); calcite and aragonite (CaCO₃). |
| ppm, ppb | Parts per million (1 ppm = 1 g/t) and parts per billion. |
| QAPF diagram | IUGS classification of plutonic rocks by quartz, alkali feldspar, plagioclase, feldspathoid. |
| Radiogenic isotope | A daughter produced by decay — ²⁰⁶Pb from ²³⁸U, ⁸⁷Sr from ⁸⁷Rb. |
| Refractive index (RI) | Speed of light in air divided by that in the gem; read on a refractometer. |
| Refractory | A material that keeps its strength at high temperature. |
| Resource / reserve | Resource: estimated quantity with reasonable prospects (inferred, indicated, measured). Reserve: the economically mineable part (probable, proven). |
| Secondary mineral | A mineral formed at the surface from the breakdown of a primary one. |
| Silicate | A mineral built from SiO₄ tetrahedra; classified by how the tetrahedra link (isolated, chains, sheets, frameworks). |
| Simulant / imitation | A different material that looks like the gem — cubic zirconia or glass for diamond. |
| Single / double refraction | Isotropic gems (cubic, amorphous) have one RI; anisotropic gems split light into two rays. |
| Sintering | Bonding powder into a dense solid by heating below the melting point. |
| Solid solution | A continuous range of compositions in one structure — olivine from Mg₂SiO₄ (forsterite) to Fe₂SiO₄ (fayalite). |
| Sorption | Attachment of ions or molecules to a mineral surface. |
| Space group | One of 230 combinations of point symmetry with translations (screw axes, glide planes). |
| Specific gravity (SG) | Density relative to water; quartz 2.65, pyrite ~5.0, gold 19.3. |
| Streak | The color of the powder, seen by rubbing on unglazed porcelain; more reliable than body color. |
| Synthetic | Laboratory-made with the same chemistry and structure as the natural gem. |
| Tailings | Fine processing waste, often stored behind dams. |
| Tailings | Finely ground waste left after processing. |
| Texture | Grain size and arrangement — phaneritic, aphanitic, porphyritic, glassy, vesicular. |
| Thin section | A rock slice ground to 30 µm and mounted on glass for the microscope. |
| Tonnage | Mass of ore in a deposit, usually in million tonnes (Mt). |
| Treatment | Any process that changes appearance — heating, diffusion, fracture filling, oiling, irradiation, coating. |
| Twinning | Two or more crystals of one species intergrown by a symmetry rule. |
| Unit cell | The smallest box that builds the crystal by translation; edges a, b, c and angles α, β, γ. |
| Weathering: physical / chemical | Breaking down by stress (frost, roots) / by reaction with water and air. |
| δ notation | Stable-isotope ratio relative to a standard, in parts per thousand (‰) — δ¹⁸O, δ¹³C. |

## Closing thought

A mineral is a **record and a resource at once**. It records the conditions it formed under, and it offers properties someone will want to use. Learn the six-layer stack, work through the eight domains, and a stone in your hand becomes a story you can read backwards (where it came from) and forwards (what it can become, and what it will leave behind).
