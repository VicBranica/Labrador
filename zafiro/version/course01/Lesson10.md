## 1. What you're looking at

*← start concrete*

Pairs that should not be pairs: a pencil and a diamond (both carbon); a stick of chalk and a pearl (both calcium carbonate); a quartz pebble and an opal (both silica); a ruby and a blue sapphire (both corundum).

## 2. The one thing to understand

*← give the mental model*

Same composition, different structure gives different properties — these are **polymorphs**. Same structure, a different trace of composition gives different colours and behaviours — ruby and sapphire are one mineral with different impurities. And the **conditions** of formation decide which structure you get: diamond needs mantle pressure, graphite does not. Composition × structure × conditions is the thread that runs through all eight piles.

## 3. What to build

*← hands on the bench*

1. Graphite vs. quartz: draw a thick, dark pencil patch on paper and measure its resistance with a multimeter across a few centimetres — it conducts. A quartz pebble does not. Same group of the periodic table, different bonding.
2. Calcite vs. aragonite: drop a piece of chalk (calcite) and a piece of seashell (mostly aragonite) into vinegar. Both fizz — same composition — but the shell holds its shape longer because of its layered structure and organic binder.
3. Quartz across the piles: on one quartz pebble, test hardness (scratches glass — mineralogy), look for conchoidal fracture (crystallography), weigh it for SG (2.65), and click a gas-lighter igniter (a manufactured piezoelectric crystal — materials science).

## 4. Key materials

*← what it's made of*

- **Polymorph sets:** C (diamond, graphite); CaCO₃ (calcite, aragonite); SiO₂ (quartz, tridymite, cristobalite, coesite, stishovite); Al₂SiO₅ (kyanite, andalusite, sillimanite); TiO₂ (rutile, anatase, brookite); FeS₂ (pyrite, marcasite).
- **Colouring ions (chromophores):** Cr³⁺ in ruby, emerald and red spinel; Fe and Ti together in blue sapphire; Fe in aquamarine and amethyst; Cu in turquoise and malachite.
- **One mineral, eight piles:** quartz — a common mineral, a chiral trigonal crystal, a silicon reservoir, a major mineral of granite and sandstone, high-purity quartz as an ore, amethyst and citrine as gems, oscillators and silicon as materials, and crystalline silica dust as a hazard.

## 5. Key techniques

*← how it's made*

- **XRD for structure, chemistry for composition** — you need both to name a phase; polymorphs have identical chemistry.
- **Raman spectroscopy** — separates polymorphs quickly and non-destructively (diamond from graphite, calcite from aragonite).
- **Phase diagrams** — maps of which structure is stable at which P and T.

## 6. The clever trick

*← what unlocked the industry*

**Chromium in two structures.** The same Cr³⁺ ion makes ruby red and emerald green. In corundum the surrounding oxygens push harder on the chromium (a stronger crystal field), shifting its absorption so red light passes; in beryl the field is weaker and the balance tips to green. One impurity, two structures, two of the most valuable colours in the gem trade — and one explanation that runs from crystallography through gemology to lasers in materials science.

## 7. Who to know

*← short lineage of names*

Mitscherlich (isomorphism and polymorphism), Goldschmidt (substitution rules), Pauling (structure rules, 1929), Bowen (phase equilibria in melts), Nassau (*The Physics and Chemistry of Color*).

## 8. The example everyone should work

*← optional — work it with pencil and paper*

Why diamonds come from deep.

1. Molar volume of graphite: 12.011 g/mol ÷ 2.26 g/cm³ = 5.31 cm³. Diamond: 12.011 ÷ 3.52 = 3.41 cm³ — about 36% smaller.
2. Pressure favours the denser phase (Le Chatelier). Experiments place the graphite–diamond boundary at roughly 4.5 GPa near 1200 °C.
3. Depth for 4.5 GPa with mantle density ~3300 kg/m³: h = P / (ρg) = 4.5 × 10⁹ / (3300 × 9.81) ≈ 139 km.
4. So natural diamonds crystallize more than about 140 km down and need a fast ride up — kimberlite eruptions — to arrive before they turn back to graphite.

Composition is the same; structure and conditions make the difference between a pencil and a gem.
