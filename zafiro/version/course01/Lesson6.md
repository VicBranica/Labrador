## 1. What you're looking at

*← start concrete*

A diamond ring and a cubic zirconia stud. A glass "gem" from a costume necklace. An amethyst slice. A pearl. A 10× jeweler's loupe — the gemologist's first instrument.

## 2. The one thing to understand

*← give the mental model*

A gem is identified by how it handles light — refractive index, birefringence, dispersion, pleochroism — together with specific gravity and what is trapped inside it. Natural, synthetic and treated stones can share the same chemistry, so the evidence is in growth: inclusions, growth lines and colour zoning record whether a crystal grew in a rock, in a laboratory, or was changed afterwards.

## 3. What to build

*← hands on the bench*

1. Learn the loupe: hold it close to your eye, bring the stone up to it, and sketch three inclusions in a quartz or amethyst. Inclusions are the gem's fingerprint.
2. The disappearing glass: drop a clear glass marble or Pyrex rod into vegetable oil. With almost the same RI (~1.47), it nearly vanishes. Relief is the gemologist's quick RI check.
3. SG of a stone: use the hydrostatic method from Mineralogy, Build 3, on a large quartz or amethyst. Expect 2.65.
4. Double refraction: put a clear calcite rhomb (Iceland spar, from a rock shop) on a printed word and turn it. Two images, one fixed, one rotating.

## 4. Key materials

*← what it's made of*

- **Diamond (C):** RI 2.417, dispersion 0.044, hardness 10, SG 3.52.
- **Corundum (Al₂O₃):** ruby (Cr) and sapphire (Fe + Ti and others); RI 1.762–1.770, SG 4.00, hardness 9.
- **Beryl (Be₃Al₂Si₆O₁₈):** emerald (Cr, V), aquamarine (Fe); RI about 1.57–1.58.
- **Quartz (SiO₂):** amethyst, citrine, rock crystal, agate; RI 1.544–1.553.
- **Opal (SiO₂·nH₂O):** packed silica spheres; RI about 1.45; play-of-colour when the spheres are ordered.
- **Others:** spinel, garnet, tourmaline, topaz, jadeite and nephrite (jade), pearl (aragonite nacre).
- **Simulants:** cubic zirconia (RI ~2.15–2.18, SG ~5.6–6.0), synthetic moissanite (SiC, RI ~2.65–2.69, doubly refractive), glass.

## 5. Key techniques

*← how it's made*

- **Cutting** — sawing, bruting, faceting, polishing — proportions decide brilliance and fire.
- **Heat treatment** — dissolves silk and shifts colour in corundum; routine and usually stable.
- **Diffusion, filling, oiling** — beryllium diffusion of sapphire, glass filling of ruby, oil or resin in emerald — must be disclosed.
- **Standard gem testing** — loupe, refractometer, polariscope, spectroscope, SG — all non-destructive.
- **Laboratory testing** — Raman, FTIR, UV–Vis–NIR, photoluminescence and LA-ICP-MS for synthetics, treatments and origin.

## 6. The clever trick

*← what unlocked the industry*

**Total internal reflection in the brilliant cut.** A high refractive index makes a small critical angle — 24.4° for diamond. Tolkowsky worked out in 1919 the pavilion angles that send light entering the top back out of the top instead of leaking through the bottom. Brilliance is geometry plus refractive index; a well-cut diamond is a light trap.

## 7. Who to know

*← short lineage of names*

Snell (refraction, 1621), Bartholin (double refraction, 1669), Church (gem spectroscopy, 1866), Verneuil (synthetic ruby, 1902), Tolkowsky (brilliant cut, 1919), Shipley (GIA, 1931), Anderson and Payne (Chelsea filter, 1934), Hall and GE (synthetic diamond, 1954).

## 8. The example everyone should work

*← optional — work it with pencil and paper*

Compare diamond (n = 2.417, SG 3.52), cubic zirconia (n ≈ 2.16, SG ≈ 5.8) and glass (n ≈ 1.50).

1. Critical angle: θc = arcsin(1/n). Diamond: arcsin(0.4137) = 24.4°. CZ: arcsin(0.463) = 27.6°. Glass: arcsin(0.667) = 41.8°.
2. The smaller the critical angle, the more light is trapped and returned — glass leaks light, which is why glass "diamonds" look dull.
3. A 1.00 ct diamond weighs 0.200 g; its volume is 0.200 / 3.52 = 0.0568 cm³.
4. A CZ of exactly the same size weighs 0.0568 × 5.8 = 0.33 g = 1.65 ct.
5. So a "1 ct" CZ that sits in a 1 ct setting will weigh about 1.6–1.7 ct on a carat scale — a quick tell.

Two numbers — RI and SG — separate diamond from its commonest simulant without a lab.
