## 1. What you're looking at

*← start concrete*

A granite kitchen countertop. Basalt "lava rocks" for a grill. A marble tile. A slate roof tile. Sandstone paving. A pumice stone from the bathroom.

## 2. The one thing to understand

*← give the mental model*

A rock is a record of where and how it formed. Igneous rocks cooled from melt, and their grain size records how fast; sedimentary rocks were deposited and cemented, and their grains record transport; metamorphic rocks recrystallized in the solid state under new pressure and temperature, and their mineral assemblage records how deep and how hot. Learn to read the minerals and the texture and the rock tells you its history.

## 3. What to build

*← hands on the bench*

1. Sort a bag of garden gravel into igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic, using grain size, layering, foliation and the vinegar test. Keep a reference tray.
2. Cooling rate in the kitchen: melt sugar into a thin sheet on baking paper and let it cool fast (glass, like obsidian); grow rock candy from a slow-cooling syrup (crystals, like granite). Hot sugar burns badly — use gloves.
3. Look at a granite countertop with a hand lens and estimate the percentage of quartz (grey, glassy), feldspar (white or pink, blocky) and mica or hornblende (dark).

## 4. Key materials

*← what it's made of*

- **Igneous:** quartz, feldspars, micas, amphiboles, pyroxenes, olivine; volcanic glass.
- **Sedimentary:** quartz sand, clay minerals, calcite and dolomite, gypsum and halite.
- **Metamorphic:** garnet, staurolite, chlorite; the Al₂SiO₅ polymorphs kyanite, andalusite and sillimanite — a pressure–temperature gauge in one formula.

## 5. Key techniques

*← how it's made*

- **Field mapping** — what rock is where, and how the contacts cut each other — the oldest technique and still the first.
- **Thin sectioning** — cut, glue to glass, grind to 30 µm; minerals become transparent and identifiable by their optics.
- **Polarized-light microscopy** — refractive index, birefringence, extinction and twinning identify minerals in place.
- **Experimental petrology** — melt and recrystallize rocks in furnaces and presses to map stability fields.
- **Geothermobarometry** — read P and T from mineral compositions in equilibrium.

## 6. The clever trick

*← what unlocked the industry*

**The 30 µm thin section (Sorby, 1858).** Grind any rock thin enough and almost every mineral turns transparent. At exactly 30 µm, quartz shows grey-white interference colours under crossed polarizers, so the quartz in every slide doubles as a thickness gauge. One slice of glass and a microscope turned rocks from lumps into readable texts.

## 7. Who to know

*← short lineage of names*

Hutton (deep time, 1788), Nicol (polarizing prism, 1828), Sorby (thin sections, 1858), Zirkel and Rosenbusch (microscope petrography), Barrow (metamorphic zones, 1893), Eskola (facies, 1915), Bowen (reaction series, 1928), Streckeisen (QAPF, 1976).

## 8. The example everyone should work

*← optional — work it with pencil and paper*

A rock was buried 20 km deep in continental crust with average density 2700 kg/m³ and a geothermal gradient of 25 °C per km.

1. Pressure: P = ρ g h = 2700 × 9.81 × 20 000 = 5.30 × 10⁸ Pa = 0.53 GPa (5.3 kbar).
2. Temperature: T ≈ 15 °C at the surface + 25 × 20 = 515 °C.
3. The Al₂SiO₅ triple point lies near 500 °C and 0.38 GPa (Holdaway, 1971). At 0.53 GPa and ~515 °C you are on the high-pressure side: the stable polymorph is kyanite.
4. Reverse it: kyanite plus garnet in a schist says the rock was buried more than about 14 km (0.38 GPa / 26.5 MPa per km).

Pressure is depth and temperature is heat flow; a single mineral can tell you both, which is why petrologists look for index minerals first.
