## 1. What you're looking at

*← start concrete*

Three things on your desk: a pebble, a pinch of salt and a ring with a stone in it. One is a rock, one is a mineral, one is a gem. The same rules describe all three.

## 2. The one thing to understand

*← give the mental model*

Everything in these courses is **atoms in an arrangement, formed under conditions, changed by processes**. Composition and structure fix the properties; pressure, temperature and fluids decide what forms; processes such as melting, weathering and mining move material from one state to the next. The eight domains are eight questions you can ask of the same specimen — what is it, how are its atoms arranged, where did its elements come from, what rock is it part of, is it worth mining, is it a gem, what can it be made into, and what happens when it meets air and water.

Each domain is climbed the same way, through six layers: vocabulary → measurement → discovery → material → technique → features. See [Module 0](index.md#module-0--foundations).

## 3. What to build

*← hands on the bench*

1. Assemble a starter kit: a 10× loupe, a fridge magnet, an unglazed tile (streak plate), a steel nail, a kitchen scale (0.01 g if you can), a bottle of white vinegar, an egg carton as a specimen tray, and a notebook.
2. Start a specimen log. For each specimen record a number, where and when you found it, and five observations: colour, luster, hardness against the nail, streak, and whether the magnet pulls it.
3. Read one specimen through all eight piles. A piece of granite: quartz, feldspar and mica (mineralogy); feldspar cleavage and twinning (crystallography); its potassium and traces of uranium (geochemistry); coarse grains from slow cooling underground (petrology); quarried as building stone (economic geology); pegmatite pockets in granite can hold topaz and tourmaline (gemology); its quartz is the raw material of glass and silicon (materials science); it weathers to kaolinite clay and sand (environmental mineralogy).

## 4. Key materials

*← what it's made of*

- **A starter collection:** the ten Mohs minerals (talc, gypsum, calcite, fluorite, apatite, orthoclase, quartz, topaz, corundum, diamond — the last as a cheap industrial grit), plus halite, pyrite, magnetite, mica and a piece of granite. Rock shops sell most of these cheaply.
- **Units you will meet everywhere:** wt% and ppm (geochemistry, economic geology), g/t (ore grade; 1 g/t = 1 ppm), carat (gems; 0.2 g), GPa and °C (petrology), Å and nm (crystallography).

## 5. Key techniques

*← how it's made*

- **Observe before you test** — look, weigh and photograph first; scratch, break and dissolve last.
- **Two independent properties** — never name a specimen from one property.
- **Record provenance** — a specimen without a locality loses most of its value to science and to the market.
- **Safety** — at home, no acid stronger than vinegar; never break, sand or saw fibrous minerals or unknown old building materials; wash your hands after handling lead or arsenic minerals; never look into a UV lamp or point a laser at anyone's eyes.

## 6. The clever trick

*← what unlocked the industry*

**Reading a solid as a record (Steno, 1669).** In his *Prodromus*, Steno argued that when one solid encloses another, the enclosing one formed later — and that the angles between crystal faces stay constant however the crystal grew. Those two ideas turned stones into records that can be read: the first step for every pile in these courses.

## 8. The example everyone should work

*← optional — work it with pencil and paper*

Three unit conversions you will use in every pile.

1. **Formula to wt%.** Chalcopyrite is CuFeS₂: 63.55 + 55.85 + 2 × 32.07 = 183.54 g/mol. Copper is 63.55 / 183.54 = 34.6 wt% of the mineral.
2. **wt% to ppm and g/t.** 1 wt% = 10 000 ppm = 10 000 g/t. An ore at 0.5% Cu is 5000 ppm Cu; a gold ore at 2 g/t is 2 ppm.
3. **Carats to volume.** A 5 ct sapphire weighs 5 × 0.2 = 1.0 g; at SG 4.00 its volume is 1.0 / 4.00 = 0.25 cm³ — about the size of a pea.

The same few conversions turn a formula into a grade, a grade into a tonnage of metal, and a weight into a size.
