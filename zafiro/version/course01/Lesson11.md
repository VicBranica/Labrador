## 1. What you're looking at

*← start concrete*

A length of copper pipe, a malachite bead and a mobile phone. The same element links all three — and the story of that element passes through every pile.

## 2. The one thing to understand

*← give the mental model*

Real specimens and real products live between piles. Follow one element from source to sink and you meet all eight domains: it is concentrated by geology, identified in minerals, explained by structure, mined and processed, sometimes cut as a gem, turned into a material, and finally released or trapped in the environment. Designing anything mineral-based — a mine, a gem purchase, a remediation plan — means reading that whole chain.

## 3. What to build

*← hands on the bench*

**Capstone: a specimen dossier.** Pick one specimen you own. Write one page with eight headings, one per domain, and answer the question each pile asks: what it is, how its atoms are arranged, where its elements came from, what rock it belongs to, whether it is or was ore, whether it is a gem, what it could be made into, and how it weathers. Add one measured number per heading where you can (SG, hardness, a d-spacing from the literature, an age, a grade, an RI, a property, a pH).

Then work the copper chain below with your own numbers.

## 4. Key materials

*← what it's made of*

- **The copper chain:** chalcopyrite (primary ore) → bornite and chalcocite (enriched zones) → malachite and azurite (oxidized zone; also ornamental stones) → copper concentrate → copper metal → pyrite-bearing tailings.
- **Where each pile meets it:** see the table in [the integration pile](../course03/09-integration.md).

## 5. Key techniques

*← how it's made*

- **The identification sequence** — observe, two properties, confirm in the lab.
- **The extraction sequence** — explore, drill, model, mine, crush, float, smelt, refine.
- **The waste sequence** — characterize (acid–base accounting), store, cover, treat, monitor.

## 6. The clever trick

*← what unlocked the industry*

**Bulk mining of low-grade ore (Jackling, 1906).** At Bingham Canyon, Daniel Jackling mined disseminated copper ore then considered too poor to work, using steam shovels and railways at a scale nobody had tried. Geology (large porphyry deposits), engineering (bulk mining), processing (later, flotation) and economics had to line up together. That integration created the modern copper industry — and its largest open pits and tailings dams.

## 7. Who to know

*← short lineage of names*

Agricola (mining, 1556), Jackling (bulk mining, 1906), Lindgren (deposit classification, 1913), Sillitoe (porphyry deposits, 1972), Singer and Stumm (acid mine drainage, 1970).

## 8. The example everyone should work

*← optional — work it with pencil and paper*

One million tonnes of ore at 0.6% Cu, 88% recovery, concentrate at 28% Cu, and 2 wt% pyrite in the tailings.

1. Contained copper: 1 000 000 × 0.006 = 6000 t.
2. Recovered: 6000 × 0.88 = 5280 t Cu.
3. Concentrate: 5280 / 0.28 = 18 860 t.
4. Tailings: 1 000 000 − 18 860 ≈ 981 000 t.
5. Acid potential of the tailings (Lesson 8): 2 wt% pyrite → 1.07% S → 33.4 kg CaCO₃ per tonne → 981 000 × 0.0334 ≈ 32 800 t of limestone equivalent needed if the tailings contain no carbonate of their own.
6. Per tonne of copper: ~186 t of tailings and ~6 t of limestone equivalent.

The grade that makes the mine pay also sets the size of the waste problem; both belong in the same calculation.

More practice: the "Try it" exercises in [Module 10](index.md#module-10--integration--capstone).
