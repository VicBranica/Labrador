## A. What you're looking at

A rusty nail. An orange-stained stream bed below an old mine. Clay cat litter (bentonite). Limescale. Garden soil. Old pipe insulation or ceiling board — look, but never break or sand it: it may contain asbestos.

## B. The one thing to understand

Minerals that formed hot and deep are out of equilibrium at the Earth's surface. Water, oxygen, carbon dioxide and microbes break them down to more stable phases — clays, iron oxides, carbonates — and along the way elements are released or trapped. How fast depends on surface area and solubility; where contaminants end up depends on sorption onto clays and iron oxides.

**Key relations:** Pyrite oxidation: FeS₂ + 7/2 O₂ + H₂O → Fe²⁺ + 2 SO₄²⁻ + 2 H⁺ · Saturation index: SI = log(IAP / K_sp) — dissolves if SI < 0, precipitates if SI > 0 · Goldich stability series: olivine weathers fastest, quartz slowest — the reverse of Bowen's series

## 1. Vocabulary

*The terms that let you reason about everything below.*

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Weathering: physical / chemical | Breaking down by stress (frost, roots) / by reaction with water and air. |
| Hydrolysis | Reaction of a silicate with water that releases cations and leaves clay. |
| Secondary mineral | A mineral formed at the surface from the breakdown of a primary one. |
| Clay minerals | Sheet silicates such as kaolinite, illite and smectite; tiny, with huge surface area. |
| Cation-exchange capacity (CEC) | How many cations a clay or soil can hold and swap; high for smectite, low for kaolinite. |
| Sorption | Attachment of ions or molecules to a mineral surface. |
| Iron oxyhydroxides | Goethite, ferrihydrite, schwertmannite — the rust-coloured sponges of the environment. |
| Acid mine (rock) drainage | Acidic, metal-rich water from oxidizing sulfides. |
| Acid potential / neutralization potential | The acid a rock can make (from its sulfide) vs the acid it can consume (from its carbonate). |
| Tailings | Fine processing waste, often stored behind dams. |
| Bioavailability | The fraction of a contaminant that organisms can actually take up. |
| Biomineralization | Minerals made by organisms — shells, bones, microbial iron oxides. |
| Carbon mineralization | Locking CO₂ into carbonate minerals by reaction with Ca-, Mg- and Fe-rich rock. |
| Mineral fibres and dusts | Asbestos, crystalline silica — hazardous when inhaled. |

## 4. Material

*What carries the property.*

- **Pyrite and other sulfides:** the source of acid rock drainage and released metals (As from arsenopyrite).
- **Calcite:** the natural neutralizer — and limestone for treatment.
- **Iron oxyhydroxides:** goethite, ferrihydrite, schwertmannite — sorb arsenic, lead and phosphate.
- **Clays:** smectite (high CEC, swelling; liners and cat litter), kaolinite (low CEC).
- **Zeolites:** clinoptilolite and others — cation sieves for ammonium and caesium.
- **Asbestos minerals:** chrysotile (serpentine) and amphiboles such as crocidolite and amosite.
- **Basalt and olivine:** Ca-, Mg- and Fe-rich rock for carbon mineralization.

## 5. Technique

*How the material is found, identified, processed or treated.*

- **Acid–base accounting** — compare acid potential with neutralization potential of waste rock.
- **Leach and column tests** — measure what a waste releases to water over time.
- **Speciation** — synchrotron X-ray absorption spectroscopy reveals the chemical form of a contaminant.
- **Geochemical modelling** — saturation indices, sorption and reaction paths with codes such as PHREEQC (USGS).
- **Remediation** — lime neutralization, constructed wetlands, permeable reactive barriers, covers on tailings.

### The clever trick

**Carbon mineralization in basalt (CarbFix).** Dissolve CO₂ in water and inject it into basalt. The acidic water dissolves calcium, magnesium and iron from the rock, and carbonate minerals precipitate in the pores. In the Icelandic pilot, more than 95% of the injected carbon was mineralized within two years (reported 2016) — weathering, the slowest arrow in geology, sped up to a timescale that matters.

### Signature techniques

Acid–base accounting; leach tests; synchrotron speciation; geochemical modelling; passive and active treatment.

## 6. Features

*What the material + technique combination gives you.*

| Function | Material | Technique | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Predict acid drainage | Waste rock | Acid–base accounting | Acid-forming or not, before mining |
| Trap metals | Iron oxyhydroxides, clays | Sorption; reactive barriers | Lower dissolved As, Pb, Cu |
| Neutralize acid | Limestone, lime | Active dosing, limestone drains | pH raised, metals precipitated |
| Seal waste | Smectite clay | Compacted liners | Very low permeability |
| Store CO₂ | Basalt, olivine-rich rock | Injection, enhanced weathering | Permanent carbonate storage |
| Assess fibre hazard | Suspect material | Polarized-light and electron microscopy | Fibre type and count |

Processes: dissolution · oxidation · hydrolysis · precipitation · sorption · biomineralization.

## D. What to build

1. Rust rates: put steel nails in three jars — tap water, salt water, vinegar — and photograph them daily for a week. Rank the corrosion and name the orange product (iron oxyhydroxide).
2. Neutralization: drop chalk into vinegar and measure the pH with red-cabbage indicator (Geochemistry, Build 2) as it rises — a limestone drain in a glass.
3. Sorption: stir a spoonful of bentonite cat litter (unscented, clumping clay) into water coloured with food dye or methylene blue, let it settle, and compare with an undisturbed control. The clay takes up the dye.
4. Swelling: put dry bentonite in a measuring cup, add water, and measure the volume a day later. That swelling is why it seals landfill liners.

## E. The example everyone should work

Waste rock contains 2 wt% pyrite (FeS₂) and 1 wt% calcite (CaCO₃).

1. Sulfur in pyrite: 64.13 / 119.98 = 53.4% S, so the rock holds 2 × 0.534 = 1.07 wt% S.
2. Acid potential: AP = 31.25 × %S = 31.25 × 1.07 = 33.4 kg CaCO₃ equivalent per tonne.
3. Neutralization potential: 1 wt% calcite = 10 kg CaCO₃ per tonne.
4. Neutralization potential ratio: NP / AP = 10 / 33.4 = 0.30.
5. A ratio below 1 means the rock can make more acid than it can neutralize: this waste is likely acid-generating and needs a plan before it is dug.

One assay for sulfur and one for carbonate predict whether a waste dump will pollute a river decades after the mine closes.
