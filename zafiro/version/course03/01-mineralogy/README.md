## A. What you're looking at

A grain of table salt (halite). A quartz pebble. A pencil (graphite). Talcum powder. A cube of pyrite, "fool's gold". The vermiculite in a bag of potting mix (an altered mica).

## B. The one thing to understand

A mineral is a naturally occurring solid with a definite chemical composition (or a defined range of compositions) and an ordered arrangement of atoms. Composition and structure fix its properties, so you identify a mineral by testing properties that cannot lie: hardness, streak, cleavage, luster and specific gravity. Color is the property beginners trust most and the one that lies most often.

**Key relations:** SG = W_air / (W_air − W_water) [dimensionless] · Mohs hardness: an ordinal scratch scale, 1 talc … 10 diamond — not proportional

## 1. Vocabulary

*The terms that let you reason about everything below.*

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mineral | A naturally occurring, usually inorganic solid with a definite composition (or range) and an ordered atomic structure. |
| Mineral species | A mineral with a unique combination of composition and structure, approved by the IMA. |
| Polymorph | Same composition, different structure — diamond and graphite (C); calcite and aragonite (CaCO₃). |
| Solid solution | A continuous range of compositions in one structure — olivine from Mg₂SiO₄ (forsterite) to Fe₂SiO₄ (fayalite). |
| Habit | The usual shape a mineral grows in — cubic, prismatic, platy, fibrous. |
| Cleavage | Breaking along flat planes of weak bonding; described by number of directions and angles. |
| Fracture | Breaking without planes — conchoidal (shell-like) in quartz and glass. |
| Luster | How a surface reflects light — metallic, vitreous, pearly, resinous, earthy. |
| Streak | The color of the powder, seen by rubbing on unglazed porcelain; more reliable than body color. |
| Hardness (Mohs) | Resistance to scratching, 1–10, by comparison with ten reference minerals. |
| Specific gravity (SG) | Density relative to water; quartz 2.65, pyrite ~5.0, gold 19.3. |
| Silicate | A mineral built from SiO₄ tetrahedra; classified by how the tetrahedra link (isolated, chains, sheets, frameworks). |
| Mineral class | Grouping by the dominant anion: native elements, sulfides, halides, oxides, carbonates, sulfates, phosphates, silicates. |
| IMA | International Mineralogical Association (founded 1958); approves new mineral species and names. |

### Who to know

Agricola (*De Natura Fossilium*, 1546), Werner (external characters, 1774), Mohs (hardness scale, 1812), Berzelius (chemical analysis), J. D. Dana (*System of Mineralogy*, 1837), the Braggs (first structures, 1913), Strunz (classification, 1941).

## 4. Material

*What carries the property.*

- **Silicates:** the bulk of the crust — feldspars (the most abundant group), quartz, micas, amphiboles, pyroxenes, olivine, garnet.
- **Carbonates:** calcite and dolomite — limestone, marble; fizz in acid.
- **Oxides:** hematite and magnetite (iron), corundum (ruby and sapphire), cassiterite (tin).
- **Sulfides:** pyrite, galena, sphalerite, chalcopyrite — metallic luster, high SG, most metal ores.
- **Native elements:** gold, copper, sulfur, graphite, diamond.
- **Halides and sulfates:** halite, fluorite, gypsum — evaporites and veins.

## 5. Technique

*How the material is found, identified, processed or treated.*

- **Hand-specimen identification** — hardness, streak, cleavage, luster, SG, magnetism, acid test — no lab needed.
- **Polarized-light microscopy** — minerals in thin section by their optical properties (see Petrology).
- **X-ray diffraction (XRD)** — the structure fingerprint; the standard confirmation of a species.
- **Electron microprobe and Raman** — composition and bonding of a spot a few micrometres across.

### The clever trick

**The Mohs scale, 1812.** Ten reference minerals — talc, gypsum, calcite, fluorite, apatite, orthoclase, quartz, topaz, corundum, diamond — and one rule: whatever scratches is harder. A fingernail (~2.5), a copper coin (~3), a steel nail or glass (~5.5) fill the gaps. Identification became something you could carry in a pocket, and it still is.

### Signature techniques

Scratch tests; streak; hydrostatic weighing; acid test; XRD; electron microprobe.

## 6. Features

*What the material + technique combination gives you.*

| Function | Material | Technique | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identify a species | Any mineral | Hardness + streak + cleavage + SG | Fast, free, non-destructive (mostly) |
| Confirm a species | Powder or crystal | XRD | Unique structural fingerprint |
| Map composition | Polished section | Electron microprobe | Micrometre-scale chemistry |
| Identify in place | Crystal, inclusion | Raman spectroscopy | Non-destructive; through glass |
| Separate by density | Mixed grains | Panning, heavy liquids | Concentrates dense minerals |

Mineral classes: native elements · sulfides · halides · oxides · carbonates · sulfates · phosphates · silicates (by tetrahedron linkage: isolated · rings · chains · sheets · frameworks).

## D. What to build

1. A streak plate: the unglazed back of a bathroom tile. Streak hematite (red-brown), pyrite (greenish-black), graphite (grey) — three dark minerals, three different powders.
2. A scratch kit: fingernail, copper coin, steel nail, glass bottle, a quartz pebble. Place five unknown minerals on the Mohs scale between two neighbours.
3. Specific gravity with a kitchen scale: weigh a stone in air, then suspended by a thread in a cup of water on the scale. Compute SG and compare with quartz (2.65).
4. The acid test: a drop of vinegar on chalk, limestone or marble fizzes (CO₂ from calcite). Quartz does not.

## E. The example everyone should work

A grey pebble from the beach weighs 26.5 g in air. Hung by a thread in a cup of water standing on the scale, the scale reading rises by 10.0 g.

1. The rise in reading is the weight of displaced water: 10.0 g, so the pebble's volume is 10.0 cm³.
2. SG = W_air / (W_air − W_water) = 26.5 / 10.0 = 2.65.
3. Quartz is 2.65, calcite 2.71, feldspars 2.55–2.76. Narrow it down with a second property: it scratches glass and does not fizz in vinegar → quartz.
4. A pyrite cube of the same volume would weigh about 50 g (SG ~5.0); gold, 193 g (SG 19.3). That is why you can feel fool's gold is not gold before you test it.

Two independent properties — here SG and hardness — name most common minerals; one is never enough.
